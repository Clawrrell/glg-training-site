Negotiation Authority & Strategy

The negotiation is decided before the phone rings. Your job is to design the plan that makes the call an execution, not an improvisation.
PA Track — Module 3

Last month, a case manager accepted a $42,000 settlement on a case worth $120,000.

The demand was $145,000. The adjuster called with a first offer of $28,000. The CM countered at $55,000 — dropping $90,000 in a single move. The adjuster moved to $38,000. The CM panicked and split the difference at $42,000. No pre-negotiation worksheet. No authority limits. No true floor. No pseudo-floor. The attorney never set the guardrails. The CM was improvising with the client\u2019s money. The client lost $78,000 because the attorney failed to design the negotiation before it started.

Section 2 · Pre-Negotiation Worksheet & Authority Design · Up to 40 pts

Design the Plan Before the Phone Rings

A CM negotiating without attorney-set parameters is improvising with the client\u2019s money. The worksheet is the guardrail.
System 4 Principle

Pre-set discipline: the negotiation is decided before the phone rings.

Every adjuster call must be preceded by a completed pre-negotiation worksheet. The attorney designs the plan; the CM executes it. The worksheet contains: facts for/against, true floor, pseudo-floor, concession increments, and walk-away trigger. Without it, the CM is making real-time decisions with no guardrails — and that is how $42,000 settlements happen on $120,000 cases.

📊 The Pre-Negotiation Worksheet — 7 Components

ComponentWhat It ContainsWho Sets It
Facts ForEvery favorable fact: clear liability, strong specials, no gaps, safety rule violation, prior incidentsAttorney
Facts AgainstEvery unfavorable fact: comparative fault risk, pre-existing conditions, treatment gaps, coverage limitsAttorney
True FloorThe actual minimum acceptable settlement — the walk-away number. Based on case value, litigation costs, client needs, and BATNAAttorney
Pseudo-FloorThe stated low — higher than the true floor. Communicated to the adjuster as the attorney\u2019s minimum. Motivates the counterparty toward their real topAttorney
Concession IncrementsPre-planned movement amounts. Each increment smaller than the last (decreasing-increment strategy). Signals approaching your limitAttorney
Walk-Away TriggerThe specific condition under which negotiation ends and litigation begins. Often tied to the specials test: if the adjuster will not clear documented out-of-pocket, file suitAttorney
CM Authority ScopeWhat the CM can do without calling the attorney: counter within defined range, accept within defined range, escalate at defined triggersAttorney

⚖️ Pseudo-Floor Strategy

The pseudo-floor is strategic misdirection. You tell the adjuster: "My client\u2019s absolute bottom is $72,000." But your true floor is $65,000. This serves two purposes:

  • Anchoring the adjuster — the adjuster now believes your floor is $72K and negotiates toward it. If they reach $72K, you have $7K of room above your actual walk-away number.
  • Protecting the true floor — the true floor is never disclosed. It exists only on the worksheet. The CM knows it; the adjuster does not.
Staff Failure Mode (Banned by Structure): No first offer call until the attorney has set facts-for/against, true floor, pseudo-floor, increments, and walk-away trigger on the pre-negotiation worksheet. The CM executes the sheet; the attorney sets it. A CM negotiating without the sheet is improvising with the client\u2019s money.

Section 3 · Strategic Accept/Counter/Reject · Up to 40 pts

The Specials Test, the 50% Rule & Ripeness

Every offer requires a strategic decision: accept, counter, or reject. The tools that drive that decision are mechanical, not intuitive.
The Decision That Changes Everything

The adjuster offered $38,000 on a case with $41,000 in documented specials. The CM accepted.

The CM thought she was being strategic — "Close to specials, and the client needs the money." But the offer failed the most basic test: it did not clear the client\u2019s documented out-of-pocket losses. The client received $38,000 gross. After attorney fees ($12,654) and expenses ($2,100), the client netted $23,246 — less than the medical bills the accident caused. The specials test would have flagged this in 10 seconds.

🔍 The Specials Test — Your First-Pass Filter

Before any other analysis, apply this binary test to every offer:

  • Does the offer clear documented out-of-pocket specials? (medical bills, lost wages, expenses)
  • If YES: Proceed to further evaluation — the offer is in a negotiable range
  • If NO: This is a litigation signal, not a negotiation starting point. But verify first — sometimes a lowball reflects an information gap (incomplete file), not a posture. Confirm the adjuster has the complete demand package before moving to litigation.

📊 The 50% Counter-Offer Rule

NEVER come down more than 50% of the gap between your demand and the adjuster\u2019s offer.

RoundTheir NumberGap50% of GapYour Minimum Counter
1$25,000 (on $75K demand)$50,000$25,000$50,000
2$35,000$15,000$7,500$42,500

This ensures you are not conceding ground faster than the adjuster. If you drop $60K while they move $15K, you have signaled weakness and destroyed your leverage.

⏳ Ripeness Assessment

Pushing for settlement before the case is ripe wastes leverage. Waiting too long burns months in zombie negotiations. Assess:

  • Is the adjuster in the realistic zone? (negotiating vs. processing)
  • Is the gap closing with each round? (movement vs. entrenchment)
  • Can the client afford to wait? (financial pressure vs. patience)
  • Would litigation produce a materially better result? (adjusted for time, cost, and risk)
Staff Failure Mode (Banned by Structure): Holding firm without a plan. Refusing to move below a number sometimes pulls the adjuster up — and sometimes burns months. Hold firm ONLY when the gap is material AND the client can afford to wait. Otherwise the specials test governs: take it or file. No zombie negotiations.

Section 4 · Bad Faith Identification & Statutory Leverage · Up to 40 pts

When Delay Becomes Bad Faith — and How to Weaponize the Deadline

Insurer delay is not always bad faith. But when it is, the attorney who documents the pattern holds the strongest card at the table.
The Silence That Speaks

The adjuster went dark for 60 days. No returned calls. No email responses. No substantive counter.

The demand was sent with a complete file: clear liability, $78,000 in specials, consistent treatment, no gaps. The adjuster acknowledged receipt and said she would "review and get back to us." That was 60 days ago. The CM called four times, emailed twice, documented every attempt. The attorney recognized the pattern: delay as a tactic to pressure the client into accepting a lowball. The attorney sent a written demand with a 7-day deadline and a bad faith referral warning. The adjuster called back within 48 hours with a $62,000 offer.

⚠️ Insurer Bad Faith Indicators

Bad faith is not just "they offered too little." It is a pattern of conduct that demonstrates the insurer is not handling the claim fairly. Watch for:

  • Unreasonable delay — no substantive response within 14 days of a complete demand, repeated across follow-up cycles
  • Refusal to explain valuation — adjuster cannot or will not articulate how they arrived at their offer when asked directly
  • Systematic undervaluation — offers consistently below specials across multiple files with the same carrier, suggesting a corporate practice rather than case-specific evaluation
  • Denial without investigation — liability denied without reviewing the complete file or conducting an independent investigation
  • Bad faith stall — adjuster requests additional documentation repeatedly, each time adding new requirements after prior ones are satisfied

📅 O.C.G.A. § 33-3-28 — Policy Limits Disclosure (Negotiation Leverage)

Under O.C.G.A. § 33-3-28 (enacted 2021, SB 426), a claimant or their attorney may send a written request to the liability insurer demanding disclosure of:

  • The name of the insurer providing liability coverage
  • The name of each insured under the policy
  • The limits of coverage available under the policy

The insurer must respond within 60 days of receiving the written request. This is a powerful tool during negotiation: knowing the policy limits before making your first demand lets you calibrate your demand strategy precisely. Without limits information, you risk demanding below policy limits (leaving money on the table) or demanding above limits with no path to collect.

⚠️ O.C.G.A. § 33-4-6 — Bad Faith Penalties

When an insurer refuses to pay a claim within 60 days of a demand, the insured or claimant may recover:

  • Penalty up to 50% of the liability (not to exceed $5,000 in some contexts)
  • Reasonable attorney's fees

The 60-day demand triggers a statutory clock. The demand must be specific: identify the claim, state the amount, and provide a basis. This is the established bad faith remedy in Georgia — distinct from common-law bad faith theories.

Precision Matters: Cite the correct statute for the correct purpose. § 33-3-28 compels limits disclosure (60-day response). § 33-4-6 creates penalty exposure for refusal to pay (60-day demand). Confusing the two or citing non-existent provisions destroys your credibility with adjusters who know the statutes better than many attorneys.

Section 5 · Advanced Frameworks & Client Counseling · Up to 40 pts

Tactical Tools & The Settlement Conversation

The adjuster call is strategy. The client call is trust. Both require a framework — and improvising either one costs the client money.
The Moment That Generates Referrals

"You told me I would get at least $26,000. The check says $30,400."

That sentence — spoken by a client who expected less and received more — is the single most powerful referral moment in the entire case lifecycle. The attorney quoted a conservative net projection when the client accepted the offer. Lien reductions came in better than expected. The client\u2019s actual take-home exceeded the quoted number by $4,400. That client does not need to be asked for referrals. She will tell her cousin, her coworker, her hairdresser. The under-promise/over-deliver discipline feeds the referral engine directly.

📉 Decreasing-Increment Bargaining

When making concessions, each concession should be smaller than the last. This signals you are approaching your limit.

  • Set your target — the number you intend to reach
  • Open at a higher anchor — your demand or initial counter
  • Concede in decreasing increments — e.g., $20K down, then $10K, then $5K, then $2,500
  • End on a specific, odd number — $84,750 rather than $85,000. Specific numbers signal you have done your math
  • At the final step, add a non-monetary concession rather than more money — this communicates "I have nothing left to give on price"

💰 Settlement Counseling — The Under-Promise Discipline

When presenting a settlement offer to the client for acceptance, always project a net-to-client figure BELOW what you believe you will achieve.

  • Calculate honestly: Gross offer minus fees, expenses, estimated provider payments after reductions
  • Quote conservatively: State a number below your honest calculation. Expect $30K, quote "at least $26K"
  • Deliver above the quote: When the actual net exceeds the quoted number, the client experiences a positive surprise
  • If actual net is BELOW what was quoted: Do NOT proceed with the disbursement call. Escalate to the attorney immediately

✅ The Three-Confirmation Protocol

Before any settlement is finalized, obtain three separate confirmations from the client:

  1. Gross settlement confirmation: "The total the insurance company is paying is $[amount]. Does that match your understanding?"
  2. Net take-home confirmation: "Your net take-home after all deductions is $[amount]. Are you clear on that number?"
  3. Proceed confirmation: "Are you comfortable moving forward, signing the documents, and getting your money released?"

A vague "yeah" is not confirmation. Wait for three clear affirmatives. A client who verbally confirms the net amount three separate times has made a real commitment — not a polite agreement to get off the phone.

Written Acceptance Required: No settlement without written client acceptance. Documented text, email confirmation, or signed memo in CasePeer — every time. "Client said yes on the phone" is how the worst outcome happens: a settled case the client disputes.

Section 6 · Ethical Boundaries & Authority Delegation · Up to 40 pts

Rules 1.4 & 1.5, CM Authority Limits & the Line

The attorney who understands the ethical boundaries protects the client AND the firm. The attorney who ignores them creates bar complaints.
The Bar Complaint That Was Preventable

The attorney decided the offer was insulting and never told the client.

An adjuster offered $12,000 on a case the attorney demanded at $85,000. The attorney considered it a waste of time and told the CM: "Just counter at $70,000 — don\u2019t bother the client with this nonsense." Six months later, the case settled for $48,000. The client learned about the $12,000 offer from a friend who knew the adjuster. The client filed a bar complaint under Rule 1.4 for failure to communicate a settlement offer. The complaint was sustained. The attorney\u2019s judgment that the offer was "insulting" did not excuse the obligation to communicate it.

⚖️ GA Bar Rule 1.4 — Duty to Communicate ALL Offers

Georgia Rule of Professional Conduct 1.4 requires prompt communication of all settlement offers to the client. This is the number one source of bar complaints in PI practice. Key principles:

  • ALL offers means ALL offers — even lowball offers the attorney considers inadequate
  • The attorney\u2019s opinion is advice, not a filter — you communicate the offer WITH context and recommendation, not in place of the offer
  • Prompt communication — within 24 hours of receipt per GLG SOP
  • Document the communication — log the date, offer amount, your recommendation, and the client\u2019s response in CasePeer

🚫 CM Authority Limits — The UPL Line

Case managers can negotiate within attorney-set parameters. They cannot exercise independent legal judgment. The line:

CMs CAN DoCMs CANNOT Do
Counter within the pre-negotiation worksheet rangeAccept or reject an offer without attorney consultation
Document all offers and counteroffers in CasePeerExercise independent judgment on settlement value
Escalate at defined triggers (per worksheet)Advise clients on whether to accept or reject
Communicate the adjuster\u2019s position to the attorneyMake final settlement authority decisions
Follow the negotiation script provided by the attorneyDeviate from the script based on their own legal analysis

A CM who exercises independent judgment on whether to accept a settlement amount is practicing law without a license. The CM documents the offer, presents it to the attorney, and the attorney makes the recommendation to the client.

💰 GA Bar Rule 1.5 — Fee Reasonableness

Fee discussions are part of settlement counseling. When the client is deciding whether to accept an offer or proceed to litigation, the fee implications are material:

  • Pre-litigation rate: 33.3% — applies to settlements reached before suit is filed
  • Post-litigation rate: 40% — applies once suit is filed
  • Material impact: On a $100,000 recovery, the difference is $6,700 ($33,300 vs. $40,000). Plus litigation expenses (depositions, expert witnesses, court costs)
  • Counseling obligation: The client must understand that rejecting the current offer and proceeding to litigation changes the economics. The net-to-client calculation shifts materially
Puffing vs. Misrepresentation: Stating your client\u2019s negotiation position ("My client will not accept less than $120,000") when the client has authorized $85,000 is legitimate puffing — advocating a position. Fabricating material facts (inventing medical records, citing non-existent expert opinions, claiming non-existent coverage) is misrepresentation — an ethics violation. The line is between POSITION and FACT.
