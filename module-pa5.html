Client Management: Attorney Level

The conversations that define whether a client trusts the firm — or fires it.
PA Track — Module 5

A client left the firm last month. Not because her case was weak. Because nobody told her the truth.

Her case was worth $35,000–$50,000. She expected $200,000. For nine months, nobody corrected that expectation. When the settlement offer came in at $38,000 — a reasonable number — she felt blindsided. She called the office furious: "Why didn't anyone TELL me?" She fired the firm, posted a one-star Google review, and filed a bar complaint. The case outcome was fine. The client management was a catastrophe. One honest conversation in month two would have prevented all of it.

📋 What You Will Learn

  • The structured conversation framework — a repeatable 5-step process for delivering difficult news to clients while maintaining trust and the attorney-client relationship
  • Fee discussions — how to explain fee structures, deliver cost updates, and handle fee disputes in compliance with Georgia Bar Rule 1.5, including the 24-hour written follow-up requirement
  • Expectation management — how to communicate realistic case timelines, value ranges, and uncertainty without destroying client confidence or violating Rule 1.4
  • Settlement conversations — walking clients through offers, explaining why an offer may be reasonable or insufficient, and ensuring informed consent
  • Case drop and withdrawal — how to ethically decline or withdraw from representation under Rule 1.16, including the retention attempt, documentation requirements, and client notification procedures
  • Client emotional management — addressing fear, anger, and frustration BEFORE attempting rational case discussion
  • The 24-hour written follow-up rule — every verbal fee or settlement discussion memorialized in writing within 24 hours
This Is NOT CM-Level Communication. Case Managers handle routine updates, treatment check-ins, and status calls. PA-5 covers the conversations that CMs escalate to attorneys — the ones that require legal judgment, ethical precision, and the authority to deliver hard truths about case value, fees, and firm decisions.

Section 2 · The Structured Conversation Framework · Up to 40 pts

Five Steps That Turn Hard Conversations Into Trust

Every difficult client conversation follows the same architecture. Master the structure and the words will come.
Real Pattern

The attorney who skipped step one lost the client in thirty seconds.

She called to tell Mrs. Davis that the insurance company denied the lost wages claim. She opened with: "I have some bad news about your lost wages." Mrs. Davis heard "bad news" and immediately went to fear mode — "Are you dropping my case? Is everything ruined?" The next twenty minutes were spent calming Mrs. Davis down from a panic that never should have started. The attorney delivered the information before she addressed the emotion. She got the sequence backwards.

⚖️ The 5-Step Difficult Conversation Framework

Use this on EVERY difficult client conversation. The steps are sequential — skipping one creates the exact problems you are trying to avoid.

1Acknowledge the Emotion. Before anything else, name what the client is feeling. "I can hear this has been stressful for you." "It sounds like you are feeling frustrated with how long this has taken." The client cannot process rational information while their emotional brain is activated. Calm the emotion first.
2Establish Mutual Purpose. Tell them WHY you are having this conversation. "I am telling you this because I want you to make the best decision for your family." "My goal is to make sure you have all the information you need." Mutual purpose creates psychological safety — the client knows you are on their side even when the news is hard.
3Share Facts Before Conclusions. Lead with evidence, not opinions. "The medical records show a three-week gap in treatment. Here is how the insurance company will use that" is better than "Your case has a problem." Facts feel fair. Conclusions feel like judgment.
4Explore Their Perspective. After you have shared the information, ask: "What are your thoughts?" "How do you see this?" "What questions do you have?" Do NOT move to solutions before the client has processed and responded. Listening here is not passive — it is diagnostic.
5Confirm Next Steps + Written Follow-Up. Every difficult conversation ends with: (a) clear next steps both sides agree on, (b) a timeline, and (c) a written summary sent within 24 hours memorializing what was discussed and decided. The written follow-up is firm policy — it exceeds the ethical minimum and protects both attorney and client.
The Sequence Matters. Steps 1 and 2 (emotion + purpose) take 60 seconds. Skipping them to "get to the point" costs 20 minutes of damage control. A client who feels heard BEFORE receiving hard news processes it rationally. A client who gets the hard news first processes it emotionally — and then everything you say after sounds like an excuse.
Direct But Caring. The standard is not "nice." It is honest. The attorney who tells a client the truth with empathy earns more trust than one who avoids hard conversations. Telling a client "your case has a weakness and here is how we address it" is caring. Avoiding that conversation and letting them discover it at settlement is not.

Section 3 · Fee Discussions & Ethical Obligations · Up to 40 pts

Rule 1.5: Fees, Costs, and the Conversations Nobody Wants to Have

Fee disputes are the #1 source of bar complaints in Georgia. The attorney who is transparent about money from day one never has this problem.
Consequence Story

The fee conversation that never happened cost the firm a client and a bar complaint.

A client signed a contingency fee agreement at intake. Nobody explained what "costs" meant separate from the fee. When the case settled for $42,000 and the client received a distribution sheet showing $4,800 in case costs deducted BEFORE the contingency split, she was shocked: "Nobody told me I would owe money on top of the attorney fee." She had been told. It was in the contract. But nobody explained it in plain language during a real conversation. A signed contract is not the same as informed consent.

⚖️ GA Bar Rule 1.5 — Fee Obligations

RequirementStandard
Communication TimingFee basis or rate communicated to client within a reasonable time after commencing representation, preferably in writing
Contingency AgreementsMust be in writing, signed by the client. Must state the method of fee calculation, including the percentage. Must explain litigation expenses and whether deducted before or after the fee
Fee ReasonablenessFees must be reasonable considering: time, complexity, results, experience, and whether the fee is fixed or contingent
DisputesWhen a dispute arises, explain the fee structure clearly in writing. Offer fee arbitration when available.

💬 Three Fee Conversations Every Attorney Must Master

  1. The Initial Explanation (Day 1): Walk the client through the fee agreement in plain language. Explain the contingency percentage, what "costs" means (filing fees, medical records, expert fees), how costs are deducted from the recovery, and what happens if the case does not settle. Use a hypothetical number: "If your case settles for $50,000, here is what the breakdown would look like."
  2. The Cost Update (During Case): When costs increase significantly (expert retention, litigation expenses), proactively inform the client. Do not let costs accumulate silently. "We retained a biomechanics expert for $3,500. Here is why this strengthens your case and how it affects your net recovery."
  3. The Settlement Distribution (At Resolution): Walk through the distribution sheet line by line. Every dollar accounted for. No surprises. If the client does not understand a line item, explain it until they do. This is where Rule 1.5 compliance either holds or breaks.
GLG Firm Policy: 24-Hour Written Follow-Up. Every verbal fee or settlement discussion must be memorialized in writing within 24 hours. This is not optional. This is not "when you get to it." This is a same-day-or-next-morning deliverable. The written follow-up protects the firm, protects the client, and creates the documentation trail that prevents the exact bar complaint described in the scenario above.

Section 4 · Expectation Management & Settlement · Up to 40 pts

The Truth About Case Value — And How to Deliver It

A client who understands realistic case value from month two accepts a reasonable offer. A client who learns the truth at settlement fires the firm.
The Expectation Gap

"My friend got $150,000 for the same thing."

Your client has $18,000 in soft-tissue specials from a rear-end collision. No surgery. No permanent injury. Gap-free treatment and clear liability. The case is worth $30,000–$40,000. The client says her neighbor got $150,000 from "the same kind of accident." What she does not know: her neighbor had a herniated disc requiring surgery, $92,000 in specials, and a commercial defendant with $1M limits. Every client who thinks their case is undervalued is comparing it to a case they do not fully understand. Your job is to close the information gap — not to promise what you cannot deliver.

📈 The Expectation Management Timeline

Expectation management is not one conversation. It is a series of calibration points throughout the case lifecycle.

Case StageExpectation ConversationKey Message
Intake (Month 1)Initial range discussion"It is too early to value your case. The value depends on treatment outcome, medical evidence, and liability. I will give you a range when we have enough information."
Mid-Treatment (Month 3–6)Emerging value indicators"Based on what we are seeing in treatment, here is a preliminary range. This could change as more records come in."
Pre-Demand (Month 6–9)Specific value range"Based on your specials, injury type, and comparable outcomes, the realistic range is $X–$Y. Our demand will be higher to allow room for negotiation."
Settlement OfferOffer analysis"The offer is $Z. Here is how it compares to your case value range and why it may or may not be reasonable."

👥 Settlement Conversation Structure

  1. Present the offer neutrally: State the number without editorial. "The insurance company has offered $32,000 to resolve your claim."
  2. Walk through the analysis: Compare to specials, injury severity, liability strength, comparable outcomes. Use the distribution sheet to show net recovery after fees and costs.
  3. Address the gap: If the offer is below expectations, explain why — with evidence, not opinion. "Your specials are $18,000. Non-surgical soft tissue cases typically settle in the 1.5 to 2 times specials range. That puts the realistic range at $27,000 to $36,000."
  4. Explain options: Accept, counter, or reject and prepare for litigation. Explain the risks and costs of each option. Be clear about what litigation involves (time, cost, trial risk).
  5. Ensure informed consent: Per Rule 1.4, the client must understand the implications of accepting or rejecting the offer. It is THEIR decision — not yours. Your job is to ensure they have the information to make it.
  6. Document: Written follow-up within 24 hours. Offer amount, analysis, client decision, next steps.
Rule 1.4 Requirement: Informed Consent. The attorney must promptly inform the client of any settlement offer and explain it sufficiently for the client to make an informed decision. Withholding an offer — even a lowball one — violates Rule 1.4. Every offer gets communicated. Every offer gets explained. The client decides.

Section 5 · Case Drop & Withdrawal · Up to 40 pts

Rule 1.16: When to End Representation — And How to Do It Right

Dropping a case poorly produces bar complaints. Dropping a case well preserves the relationship, the firm\u2019s reputation, and the client\u2019s dignity.
Consequence Story

The case that was dropped without the retention attempt.

A paralegal flagged a case for drop: 190-day treatment gap, three unanswered calls. The CM marked it "ready for drop" and sent the drop letter. Two days later, the client called in tears. "My mother died. I was in Alabama for three months. I tried to call but the number I had was wrong." Her injuries were real. Her case was viable. She had a legitimate reason for the gap. Nobody made the retention attempt call. The SOP requires a final outreach by the supervising attorney with a 14-day response window — before any drop decision.

📋 Case Drop Policy — Decision Framework

Criterion CategoryExamplesApproval Authority
Primary (Objective)180+ day treatment gap, 3 consecutive contact attempts over 45+ days with no response, fraud/misrepresentation, SOL risk with inadequate developmentCM + Supervising Attorney
Secondary (Judgment)Adverse medical development eliminating theories, unreasonable expectations after 3+ documented education conversations, resource investment exceeding $10,000 with insufficient probable recoveryHarrell review required
Complex/EthicsConflict of interest, cases with ethics implicationsHarrell + ethics consultation

⚖️ The Drop Conversation — 5-Step Structure

1Share Facts: "Our file shows no medical treatment in the past 8 months and no response to our communications since March."
2Explain Impact: "This suggests the injuries may have resolved, which affects case value."
3Ask Their Side: "Help me understand what has been happening on your end."
4State Position Tentatively: "It appears that continuing representation may not serve your interests."
5Invite Response: "What are your thoughts on how we should proceed?"
Never Say: "Your case is worthless." "You haven\u2019t cooperated." "We don\u2019t want your case." These are factual statements that feel like judgments. Use: "We have reached a point where we cannot effectively advance your case, but we want to ensure you understand all your options."
Rule 1.16 Compliance Checklist: 30-day written notice (certified mail) • Return unearned fees within 15 days • File available for client retrieval for 30 days post-notice • SOL date communicated to client • Referral resources provided • If case filed: motion to withdraw within 10 days.

Section 6 · Client Emotional Management & Escalation · Up to 40 pts

Feelings Before Facts — And What Happens When You Get It Wrong

A client who feels heard will consider a reasonable offer. A client who feels ignored will reject it on principle.
Consequence Story

The phone call that turned a frustration into a bar complaint.

Mr. Thompson called to express concern about his case timeline — seven months and no settlement. The attorney responded: "These things take time. I understand you are frustrated, but we need to be patient." Mr. Thompson heard: "Your feelings are invalid and I am dismissing them." He called back two days later. This time he mentioned "talking to another attorney." That is a Tier 3 trigger. But the first call was never logged. The escalation was never routed. A Tier 1 complaint that could have been resolved with one empathetic conversation became a Tier 3 emergency because the attorney treated the emotion as an obstacle instead of information.

🇧🇷 Why Emotions Come First

When a client is experiencing fear, anger, or frustration, the emotional brain is in control. The rational brain — the one that processes settlement analysis, fee structures, and legal strategy — is offline. You cannot reason with a person whose emotional brain is activated. You must address the emotion FIRST.

  • Labeling: Name the emotion you observe. "It sounds like you are frustrated that this has taken so long." Labeling an emotion begins to defuse it. The client feels seen.
  • Mirroring: Repeat the last few words the client said, with an upward inflection. Client: "I just feel like nobody cares about my case." You: "Nobody cares about your case?" This invites elaboration without judgment.
  • Calibrated Questions: Open-ended questions that begin with "what" or "how" and give the client a sense of control. "What would make you feel better about how this is progressing?" "How would you like me to keep you updated going forward?"

Tier 1Service & CommunicationNo callback in 3+ weeks, confusion about case statusSame-day acknowledgment, 48-hour resolution
Tier 2Case Strategy & ExpectationsDisagreement with settlement recommendation, fee questionsSame-day acknowledgment, 48h attorney consult, 72h plan
Tier 3Serious EscalationMentions bar complaint, hiring another attorney, negative review posted, demands file transfer2-hour Harrell notification, same-day attorney response, 24h plan

These are not judgment calls. Any one of these = Tier 3, Harrell notified within 2 hours.
