Section 2 · The Four Litigation Triggers · Up to 40 pts

When Negotiation Ends and Filing Begins

Not every stalled case is a failed case. But four specific triggers mean the conversation with the carrier is over.

⚖️ The Four Litigation Triggers

TriggerDefinitionCM Action
ImpasseThe adjuster's ceiling cannot reach the attorney's floor; no settlement is possible at acceptable terms through negotiationEscalate to attorney with full negotiation history; attorney decides: file, Holt demand, or hold
SOL PressureGeorgia 2-year statute of limitations (O.C.G.A. § 9-3-33) is approaching — typically 45+ days away triggers an attorney review; inside 30 days is a crisisImmediately flag to attorney — today, not end of week; attorney must authorize filing or tolling
Coverage DenialCarrier formally denies coverage (exclusion, misrepresentation, lapse) — removes the ability to negotiate with that carrierEscalate to attorney; evaluate UM/UIM claims, alternate defendants, file against driver personally
Bad FaithCarrier refuses to fairly evaluate a clear liability case with significant damages within policy limits — creates statutory bad faith exposure under Georgia law (O.C.G.A. § 33-4-6)Escalate to attorney; attorney evaluates Holt demand to create bad faith record
Inline Primer — Statute of Limitations (SOL): In Georgia, most personal injury claims must be filed within 2 years of the date of injury (O.C.G.A. § 9-3-33). If a lawsuit is not filed before this deadline, the client permanently loses the right to sue — regardless of how strong the case is. The SOL does not stop because you are in active negotiations. The CM's job is to flag SOL proximity to the attorney with enough time to file.
Inline Primer — Bad Faith: Under O.C.G.A. § 33-4-6, an insurance carrier that refuses to pay a valid claim within 60 days of a proper demand can be liable for the full judgment amount plus attorney fees and a 50% penalty. "Bad faith" in PI practice typically describes a carrier that offers pennies on a clear liability case with severe injuries sitting inside policy limits — refusing to move toward limits without justification. The attorney must evaluate and authorize the bad faith strategy.
The Filing Decision Belongs to the Attorney — Always: The CM identifies triggers, escalates promptly, and documents. The CM does not decide to file, does not issue Holt demands without authorization, and does not tell the client "we're suing them." Every word on litigation strategy comes from the attorney.

Section 3 · Pre-Handoff File Audit · Up to 40 pts

The 8 Items Every File Must Have Before Handoff

The litigation team does not clean up pre-lit gaps. Every missing item becomes their problem — under deposition pressure, under SOL pressure, under trial pressure.

📋 Pre-Handoff File Audit Checklist — 8 Required Items

The CM completes this audit BEFORE the handoff file is sent. Every item must be verified. A handoff file with gaps is returned — not accepted.

  1. All medical records received — every treating provider, every visit, every bill. No partial collections.
  2. HIPAA authorizations signed for every provider — current, not expired (authorizations older than 12 months must be renewed).
  3. Demand letter confirmed sent and received — logged in CasePeer with adjuster name, receipt date, and method of delivery.
  4. Complete offer/counter history in CasePeer — every offer and counter, every date, every adjuster name; this is the litigation team's negotiation record.
  5. All liens documented — hospital liens, health insurance subrogation, ERISA claims; each lien has a confirmed amount and a responsible party logged.
  6. Signed client fee agreement on file — confirms the retainer was executed and the client understood the fee structure including the litigation rate.
  7. Client authorization to file (written) — the client explicitly authorized filing in writing; verbal is not sufficient.
  8. Policy limits confirmed — both the at-fault carrier's BI limits and the client's own UM/UIM limits documented with affidavit or coverage declaration.
The Litigation Team Is Not a Cleanup Crew: If a file arrives without signed HIPAA authorizations for two of five providers, the litigation team cannot request updated records or billing supplements without starting the authorization process over — during discovery, under deadline pressure. Every gap the CM leaves creates emergency work for the team that files the case.
Demand on File Is Not Optional: A case with no demand on file means the carrier was never given a formal opportunity to settle pre-litigation. Most litigation-track attorneys will not accept a file without a logged demand — and if a case reaches trial without a demand, certain fee-shifting and bad faith arguments are weakened or unavailable.

Section 4 · CasePeer Transitions & Templates · Up to 40 pts

Status Flow, Template #20, Template #117 & the Fee Change

Two templates. One status change. One fee acknowledgment. All four must happen in the right order before the file leaves the CM's hands.

🔄 CasePeer Status Flow — Litigation Transition

StatusTrigger / Required Action
Settlement NegotiationsActive pre-lit negotiation with carrier; CM owns the file
→ Pending LitTriggered ONLY after: (1) attorney authorizes filing, (2) pre-handoff file audit is 100% complete, (3) Templates #20 and #117 are sent/signed, (4) litigation team formally accepts the file
Pending LitLitigation team owns the case; CM is support-only; complaint drafting begins

📄 Template #20 — Litigation Handoff Letter (to Client)

Purpose: Notify the client that their case is moving to litigation. This is the client's orientation document — everything they need to understand about the transition.

Contains: (1) Clear statement that a lawsuit will be filed; (2) What litigation means for their case timeline; (3) The fee change — 33.3% pre-lit → 40% lit — explained in plain language; (4) Who their new point of contact is (litigation team member); (5) Next steps and expected timeline for first contact from litigation team.

Recipient: Client only — not the carrier.

📄 Template #117 — Litigation Fee Acknowledgment (Client Signature Required)

Purpose: Document the client's informed consent to the fee change from 33.3% to 40% upon filing of a lawsuit.

Required elements: Specific current fee rate (33.3%), specific new rate (40%), dollar impact example at estimated settlement value, client signature, and date.

Rule: Template #117 must be signed BEFORE the handoff file is submitted. A handoff without a signed Template #117 is incomplete and will be returned.

💰 The Fee Change — 33.3% → 40%

Pre-litigation fee: 33.3% — applies when no lawsuit has been filed

Litigation fee: 40% — applies the moment a complaint is filed in court

On a $100,000 settlement: pre-lit fee = $33,300; lit fee = $40,000. That $6,700 difference belongs to the client if they settle before filing. Every client conversation about whether to accept or litigate must include this math — clearly, honestly, in writing.

Verbal acknowledgment is not enough: A client who says "yeah, the attorney mentioned the fee changes" — without a signed Template #117 — has not given documented consent. The fee change must be in writing, signed by the client, before the file moves to the litigation team. No exceptions.

Section 5 · CM Role Post-Handoff · Up to 40 pts

Support, Not Lead — Where the Line Is

The CM doesn't disappear after handoff. They become a resource. The distinction matters — because the line between support and interference has real consequences.

⚖️ CM Authority After Handoff — What the CM Does vs. Does Not Do

CM Continues to DoCM Does NOT Do After Handoff
Answer client calls and provide emotional supportMake case strategy decisions of any kind
Explain the handoff process and next steps to the clientEvaluate settlement offers or advise acceptance/rejection
Pull additional records when requested by litigation teamNegotiate with the carrier (any contact)
Assist with documentation requests from litigation attorneyCommit the firm to any position, timeline, or number
Coordinate client scheduling for depositions or meetingsTell the client what the case is worth or what a settlement number means
Route client questions to litigation attorneyAnswer legal strategy, discovery, or deposition questions without litigation attorney input
Why "Support Not Lead" Matters: The litigation attorney has a different strategy, different case theory, and different negotiation posture than the pre-lit CM had. When the CM continues giving clients case-strategy opinions post-handoff, it creates conflicting advice, undermines the litigation attorney's positioning, and can expose the firm to ethics issues. The CM's post-handoff job is to be a bridge — not a parallel lane.
Client Communication During Transition — The Anxiety Gap: Most clients feel abandoned in the 24–72 hours after handoff. They were used to the CM. The litigation team hasn't called yet. The CM's job is to close the anxiety gap — acknowledge the transition, give a realistic timeline for first contact, and offer a safety net: "If you don't hear from the litigation team by [specific date], call me and I'll make sure someone connects with you."

Section 6 · Common Handoff Failures & Fee Acknowledgment · Up to 40 pts

The Three Failures That Keep Happening — and Why They Must Stop

Every handoff failure creates litigation team emergencies. These three appear on the same files, time after time. The system exists to prevent them.

🚫 The Three Common Handoff Failures — Banned by GLG's System

#FailureWhat It Causes
1Incomplete Records — one or more treating providers' records not collected before handoffEmergency records retrieval under deposition deadline; case weakened by documentation gaps; defense advantages the CM created
2Missing Authorizations — HIPAA forms not obtained or expired for providers with records in the fileLitigation team cannot request updated records, billing supplements, or treatment notes; authorization process must restart mid-discovery
3No Demand on File — case moves to litigation without a documented demand and receipt confirmationCarrier was never given a formal pre-litigation opportunity to resolve; weakens fee-shifting arguments; potential malpractice exposure; handoff is rejected

✍️ Fee Acknowledgment Standards

The rule is absolute: Template #117 must be signed before the handoff file is submitted. There is no exception for:

  • Verbal acknowledgment (even attorney-to-client)
  • CasePeer note documenting "client was told about fee change"
  • The fee change being "already in the retainer agreement" — the retainer covers it structurally but Template #117 is the specific written acknowledgment at the point of decision
  • Time pressure — a SOL deadline does not eliminate the requirement; it just means the CM needs to get it signed faster
Why Written Acknowledgment Protects the Client (Not Just the Firm): A client who learns mid-litigation that their fee went up without signed acknowledgment has grounds to dispute the fee. That dispute disrupts the case, creates a conflict of interest, and may require the attorney to withdraw — leaving the client without representation mid-lawsuit. Template #117 protects the client from that outcome just as much as it protects the firm.

Inline Primer — ERISA Subrogation: When a client's health insurance is provided through a large employer's self-funded plan, it is governed by federal ERISA law — not Georgia state law. This matters because Georgia's Made Whole Doctrine (which can reduce or eliminate a health insurer's right to be repaid at settlement) does not apply to self-funded ERISA plans. The plan can recover its full amount even if the client is not "made whole." The CM must flag ERISA plans in the lien documentation before handoff so the litigation team knows the correct tool: the Common Fund Doctrine, which may allow a reduction proportional to the attorney's work securing the recovery.
