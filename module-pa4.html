Section 1 · Welcome

Litigation Decision-Making

The single highest-stakes judgment call in pre-lit: when to file suit, when to settle, and how to counsel the client through the decision.
PA Track — Module 4

She settled for $38,000. The case was worth $185,000.

The client had a confirmed herniated disc at C5-C6, consistent treatment, clear liability, and $52,000 in medical specials. The insurer offered $38,000. The pre-lit attorney accepted because she was uncomfortable recommending litigation — she had never counseled a client through the fee change conversation, and she was afraid the client would refuse the 40% fee agreement. The insurer knew this. They offered 20 cents on the dollar because they calculated that the attorney would flinch. She did. Filing suit with a demand of $185,000 would have forced the insurer to the table within 90 days — the case had a Safety Checklist score of 8/10. Instead, the client got $38,000 minus fees and liens, netting roughly $12,000 for a life-altering spinal injury.

Section 2 · Litigation Triggers · Up to 40 pts

The Five Conditions That Move a Case to Litigation

No subjective “maybe we should file.” One of these conditions is met, or the case stays in pre-lit.
Real Pattern

The adjuster has been “reviewing the file” for four months. Your demand is collecting dust.

You sent a $95,000 demand on a clear-liability rear-end with $31,000 in specials and an MRI-confirmed herniated disc. The adjuster acknowledged receipt, then went silent. Four follow-up calls. Two confirming emails. Nothing. The adjuster is not evaluating the claim — they are running the clock, hoping you will either drop the demand or that the client will get frustrated and settle for pennies. This is Trigger #3: adjuster is stonewalling. File suit.

⚖️ The Five Litigation Triggers (Handoff SOP §2)

A case moves to litigation when one or more of these conditions are met:

1Demand sent and best offer is insufficient. You sent the demand, the adjuster responded, and after good-faith negotiation their best offer does not adequately compensate the client. The gap between their top number and your floor cannot be bridged through further negotiation.
2Statute of limitations requires filing. The SOL countdown (Rule B) has reached the 30-day checkpoint. A complaint must be drafted and filed regardless of negotiation status. Missing this deadline = malpractice exposure.
3Attorney determination that liability requires discovery, or adjuster is stonewalling. The case needs formal discovery (depositions, interrogatories, document requests) to establish liability, or the adjuster has refused to engage in good-faith negotiation. Silence IS stonewalling.
4Client authorization after settlement counseling. The client has been fully informed of the litigation process, fee change, and timeline — and has authorized filing suit. This trigger requires the counseling conversation to have occurred first.
5PITT review flag. The case was flagged during attorney review for litigation escalation based on case posture, insurer behavior, or strategic value of filing.
Trigger Recognition Is Not Optional. When a trigger is present, the analysis shifts from “should we file?” to “what is the cost-benefit of filing?” A present trigger does not mean automatic filing — it means the attorney MUST evaluate the litigation decision using the framework in Section 3. Ignoring a present trigger is as dangerous as filing prematurely.

🚨 Bad Faith as a Litigation Catalyst

Insurer bad faith creates a distinct category of litigation triggers. When the insurer:

  • Refuses policy limits on a clear-liability case with damages exceeding coverage
  • Lowballs with no documented justification (“we value the case at $5,000” on a $75,000 demand with $31,000 in specials)
  • Delays response beyond 30 days with no substantive engagement
  • Denies causation despite uncontradicted medical evidence

These behaviors indicate the insurer is not negotiating in good faith. Filing suit becomes both a legal strategy (access to discovery, court oversight) and a leverage tool (the insurer must now allocate defense resources and face real exposure).

Section 3 · Suit vs. Settle Cost-Benefit · Up to 40 pts

The Math Behind the Decision

Litigation is justified when expected recovery minus costs exceeds the current settlement offer — adjusted for time value and collection risk.
The Numbers That Matter

The insurer offered $42,000. Your attorney gut says “we can do better.” Your analysis should say something more precise.

Case value estimate: $150,000. Safety Checklist: 7/10 (average — not a strong trial case, but viable). Probability of success at trial: approximately 65%. Litigation costs: filing fees ($250), two depositions ($6,000), one expert witness ($12,000), mediation ($3,000) = $21,250 in incremental costs. Expected recovery: $150,000 × 65% = $97,500. Net expected recovery after costs: $97,500 − $21,250 = $76,250. The settlement offer is $42,000. The math says file. But add 18 months of delay and the fee change from 33.3% to 40%, and the client\u2019s net-to-client calculation changes too.

📊 The Cost-Benefit Framework

The Decision Equation:

(Case Value × Probability of Trial Success) − Litigation Costs > Current Settlement Offer

If the left side exceeds the right side, litigation is mathematically justified. But the math is only the starting point — time value, collection risk, and client circumstances also factor in.

🎯 Safety Checklist Score as Trial Predictor

The Case Safety Checklist score (from PA-1) is not just an intake tool — it directly feeds the probability-of-success variable in the cost-benefit equation.

ScoreTrial PostureLitigation Decision Impact
8–10Strong trial caseLitigation is leverage. Filing often compels settlement before trial because the insurer knows it will lose. Probability of trial success: 70–85%.
5–7Average caseLitigation is a tool if negotiation stalls. Must weigh costs carefully. Probability of trial success: 50–65%.
0–4Weak trial postureSettle unless insurer is acting in bad faith. Litigation risk exceeds potential gain in most scenarios. Probability of trial success: 25–45%.
Cognitive Biases That Corrupt the Decision. Anchoring: Do not anchor to the demand amount or the insurer\u2019s first offer when evaluating case value — use the specials-based valuation and safety checklist independently. Sunk cost fallacy: “We have already invested so much in this case” is not a reason to file suit — evaluate the forward-looking cost-benefit only. Loss aversion: Do not settle cheap because you fear losing at trial more than you value winning. Overconfidence: Do not file weak cases because you overestimate trial success probability. Use the Safety Checklist score, not your gut.

Section 4 · SOL Management · Up to 40 pts

Rule B Countdown & Filing Deadlines

A missed statute of limitations is malpractice. Period. The countdown system exists to make that impossible.
Malpractice Exposure

The SOL was March 15. The complaint was filed March 18. The case is dead.

The client was injured on March 15, 2024. The 2-year SOL under O.C.G.A. § 9-3-33 expired on March 15, 2026. The pre-lit attorney knew the deadline was approaching but was waiting for the adjuster to respond to a final counter. The adjuster never responded. By the time the attorney checked the calendar, three days had passed. No complaint had been drafted. No complaint had been filed. The claim is time-barred. The client has no case. And the firm has a malpractice claim.

📅 Rule B — The SOL Countdown System

Rule B creates three mandatory checkpoints before the SOL expires. Missing any checkpoint is a corrective action trigger.

CheckpointDays Before SOLRequired ActionOwner
Flag90 daysCase evaluation complete, demand finalized or sent, litigation decision analysis begunPre-Lit Attorney (Adriauna)
Pending Lit60 daysClient counseling conversation completed, fee agreement sent, Handoff SOP Steps 1–3 in progressPre-Lit Attorney + CM
Complaint Filed30 daysComplaint drafted, reviewed, and filed with court. Fee agreement must be signed.Attorney (Adriauna → Harrell approval → Dmitri)
The 30-Day Checkpoint Is Not a Target — It Is a Safety Net. A complaint filed at 30 days before SOL means something went wrong upstream. Ideally, the litigation decision is made well before the 90-day checkpoint, the client is counseled at 60 days, and the complaint is filed with margin. The 30-day checkpoint exists to catch cases that fell through the cracks — not as a normal workflow target.
Venue Selection (O.C.G.A. § 9-10-31): When filing, choose venue strategically. Georgia allows suit in the county where the defendant resides, where the cause of action arose, or where the defendant may be served. Venue affects jury pool demographics, which affects expected recovery and settlement posture. Discuss venue strategy with Harrell before filing.

Section 5 · Client Counseling on Litigation Risk · Up to 40 pts

The Conversation That Changes Everything

The “should we file suit?” conversation is high stakes, high emotion, and high consequence. Handle it wrong and you lose the client. Handle it right and you earn their trust for the next 18 months.
Real Pattern

“You want to take MORE of my money and make me wait TWO YEARS?”

The client heard “fee change” and immediately assumed the firm was trying to profit at her expense. She did not hear the part about the $95,000 gap between the insurer\u2019s offer and the case value. She did not hear that filing suit often compels settlement within 90 days. All she heard was “40%” and “12 to 24 months.” The attorney panicked, said “let me check with my boss,” and the client called three other firms before the end of the day. The counseling conversation failed because the attorney led with the bad news instead of establishing mutual purpose first.

💬 The Counseling Conversation Framework

This conversation has a specific structure. Do not improvise.

1Establish mutual purpose. “My recommendation is based on one thing: getting you the best possible outcome. Everything I am about to explain is in service of that goal.” The client must believe you are on their side BEFORE you deliver any information that might trigger anxiety.
2Present the current situation honestly. “The insurer offered $30,000. Based on your injuries, treatment, and the strength of your case, I believe your case is worth $130,000 to $150,000. There is a $100,000 gap between what they are offering and what your case is worth.”
3Explain the options. “We have two paths. Path A: accept the $30,000. Path B: file a lawsuit, which gives us access to formal discovery, court oversight, and significantly more leverage. Most cases settle after suit is filed — we are not necessarily going to trial.”
4Address the fee change directly. “If we file suit, the fee structure changes from 33.3% to 40%. I want to be completely transparent about that. The reason for the change is that litigation requires significantly more resources — filing fees, depositions, expert witnesses, and 12 to 24 months of additional attorney work.”
5Show the net-to-client comparison. Run both scenarios: “If we accept $30,000 now at 33.3%, after fees and liens your take-home is approximately $X. If we litigate and recover $130,000 at 40%, after fees, costs, and liens your take-home is approximately $Y.” The client needs concrete numbers, not abstract percentages.
6Set realistic expectations. Timeline (12–24 months), process (discovery, depositions, possible mediation, possible trial), and uncertainty (no guaranteed outcome). Under-promise, over-deliver.
7Obtain informed authorization. “This is YOUR decision. I have given you my professional recommendation and the analysis behind it. What would you like to do?” Per Rule 1.2, the client decides.
GA Bar Rule 1.4 (Communication). The attorney MUST keep the client reasonably informed about the status of the case and must explain matters to the extent reasonably necessary for the client to make informed decisions. The litigation recommendation IS that informed decision. Failure to fully inform the client of risks, costs, and alternatives before seeking authorization to file violates Rule 1.4.
GA Bar Rule 1.5 (Fees). The fee change from 33.3% to 40% requires a new written agreement with full disclosure. The client must understand the change, the reason for the change, and the impact on their recovery. The fee agreement (Template 120) must be signed before litigation work begins.

Section 6 · Government Entity Special Analysis · Up to 40 pts

When the Defendant Is the Government

Different defendant, different rules, different deadlines, different math. The litigation decision for government entities is fundamentally different from private defendants.
Claim Barred

The client was hit by a MARTA bus. The case was worth $500,000. The ante litem notice was never sent.

The client suffered a catastrophic knee injury requiring two surgeries when a MARTA bus ran a red light. Clear liability. Strong damages. But no one at the firm recognized MARTA as a municipal entity requiring an ante litem notice. The 6-month deadline passed silently. When the pre-lit attorney checked the file at 8 months post-accident, the claim was already barred. No complaint can fix this. No amount of strong liability or devastating injuries can resurrect a claim killed by a missed ante litem deadline. The ante litem notice IS the litigation decision for government cases — and the decision must be made months earlier than for private defendants.

🏛️ Georgia Tort Claims Act — Ante Litem Notice Requirements

Entity TypeDeadlineStatuteKey Requirement
Municipal (city, county, MARTA)6 monthsO.C.G.A. § 36-33-5Written notice with specific dollar amount of damages claimed
State entity12 monthsO.C.G.A. § 50-21-26Written notice with facts and dollar amount

Critical distinction: The ante litem deadline is NOT a filing deadline — it is a PREREQUISITE to filing. Missing it does not shorten your time to file; it eliminates your right to file entirely, regardless of how much SOL remains.

🛡️ Sovereign Immunity Analysis

  • General rule: Georgia government entities enjoy sovereign immunity unless the state has waived it by statute
  • Waiver for torts: The Georgia Tort Claims Act (O.C.G.A. § 50-21-20 et seq.) waives sovereign immunity for certain torts committed by state employees acting within the scope of employment
  • Discretionary function exception: The state retains immunity for “discretionary acts” (policy decisions, resource allocation). The distinction between discretionary (immune) and ministerial (potentially liable) functions is the central litigation question for government cases
  • Damage caps: Government entity cases may have statutory damages limitations that fundamentally change the cost-benefit calculation
Government Defendant Recognition Is an Intake Function. By the time the case reaches the pre-lit attorney, the government defendant should already be identified. But double-check: Was the at-fault driver working for a government entity? Was the property government-owned? Was the vehicle government-owned? If any answer is yes, the ante litem clock is ticking from the date of injury — not from the date you identified the government connection.
